        "content_html": "<p>The motivating question — why a coronary laceration kills within minutes while an equivalent arterial injury elsewhere allows hours for intervention — finds its answer in the convergence of three anatomically unique characteristics of the heart: (1) the myocardium already extracts near-maximal oxygen from coronary blood at rest (~70% extraction, versus ~25% in skeletal muscle), leaving no extraction reserve to compensate for reduced flow; (2) the absence of functionally significant coronary anastomoses in most individuals prevents immediate collateral circulation; and (3) coronary flow is diastolic, and any compensatory tachycardia shortening diastole worsens ischemia at the very moment the heart most needs oxygen.</p>\n\n<p>There is a profound tension in cardiac anatomy between robustness and vulnerability: the heart is the only organ that cannot stop functioning even for a fraction of a second to be supplied, and paradoxically is the only one that interrupts its own supply with every systole. The evolutionary solution — diastolic flow through the sinuses of Valsalva — is elegant but creates dependence on aortic diastolic pressure and diastolic duration. The entire edifice of coronary pathology, from cardiogenic shock to transmural infarction, is a logical consequence of this anatomical equation.</p>\n\n<p>An implication that emerges from the data but is rarely stated explicitly: the anatomy of the cardiac surfaces and coronary territories creates a map of predictability that transforms the ECG into an instrument of anatomical localization before it is a diagnostic instrument. The lead showing ST elevation is the electrical window into the ischemic surface; the ischemic surface belongs to a specific coronary territory; the territory identifies the culprit vessel by its occlusion pattern. Anatomy, electrophysiology, and clinical medicine are, in this field, a single language.</p>",
